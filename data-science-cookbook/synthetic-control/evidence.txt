SYNTHETIC CONTROL: one market, one intervention, a control group that has to be built
Day 169 - data-science-cookbook - phoebe-the-builder

==============================================================================
 1. THE INSTRUMENT WORKS: one treated unit, no control group, a fitted one
==============================================================================
A single market got the intervention.  There is no control group, so the three things
people reach for are the closest-looking single market, the average of the others, and a
difference-in-differences against that average.  Synthetic control instead asks: which
WEIGHTED average of the others tracked this market before the intervention?

The panel below is an interactive fixed-effects model - each unit loads on three common
time factors - and the treated unit's loadings are a lopsided convex combination of the
donors', so it is genuinely an unusual member of its own pool.  A convex combination that
reproduces it exists; the estimators differ in whether they can find it.

  600 panels, 20 donors, 30 pre-periods, 12 post, true effect 5.0

  estimator            mean     bias     rmse   x synth rmse
  synthetic           4.973   -0.027    0.577           1.00
  diff-in-diff        5.069    0.069    1.214           2.10
  donor average       5.105    0.105    1.841           3.19
  closest donor       4.849   -0.151    1.486           2.58

  The fit is sparse by construction: 8.4 donors carry non-zero weight out of 20,
  the largest one carries 0.29 of the total. That sparsity is the simplex constraint,
  not a penalty anyone chose - it is what section 6 removes.

==============================================================================
 2. THE P-VALUE HAS A FLOOR, AND THE FLOOR IS THE DESIGN, NOT THE DATA
==============================================================================
Inference is a permutation: refit the estimator with each donor playing the treated
unit, and rank the real one.  With N units there are N ranks, so the smallest p-value the
procedure can return is 1/N = 1/(donors+1).  No effect size changes this.  No sample size
changes it either - it is arithmetic on the donor count, available before any data.

    donors   floor 1/(J+1)   0.05 reachable  0.10 reachable
         5          0.1667               NO              NO
         9          0.1000               NO             yes
        10          0.0909               NO             yes
        15          0.0625               NO             yes
        19          0.0500              yes             yes
        20          0.0476              yes             yes
        39          0.0250              yes             yes
        50          0.0196              yes             yes

  19 donors is the smallest pool that can ever produce p <= 0.05, and 9 the smallest
  that can produce p <= 0.10.  A study of 12 US states cannot report a 5% result.

  Power of the placebo test, 400 panels each (rejection at the nominal level):

    donors   effect   p<=0.05   p<=0.10   median p
        10      0.0     0.000     0.048      0.545
        10      2.0     0.000     0.623      0.091
        10      5.0     0.000     1.000      0.091
        10     10.0     0.000     1.000      0.091
        20      0.0     0.077     0.140      0.429
        20      2.0     0.710     0.787      0.048
        20      5.0     0.998     0.998      0.048
        20     10.0     1.000     1.000      0.048
        40      0.0     0.068     0.125      0.390
        40      2.0     0.780     0.868      0.024
        40      5.0     1.000     1.000      0.024
        40     10.0     1.000     1.000      0.024

  The 0.05 column is exactly 0.000 for 10 donors at EVERY effect size, including one
  twice the size that 40 donors detects with certainty.  That is not low power, it is
  zero power: the test has no outcome that rejects.  Under the null (effect 0.0) the
  0.10 column sits at the floor's granularity, which is what calibration looks like
  when the p-value can only take J+1 values.

==============================================================================
 3. NEGATIVE: THE PRE-PERIOD FIT IS THE THING EVERYONE SHOWS, AND IT IS NOT EVIDENCE
==============================================================================
Every synthetic control paper opens with the pre-period plot: two lines lying on top of
each other.  It is offered as the credential.  But the weights were CHOSEN to make those
lines coincide, so the fit is a measure of how much freedom the optimiser had, and the
freedom grows with the donor count while the accuracy does not.

  10 pre-periods held fixed, donor pool grown, 500 panels each:

    donors   pre-RMSPE   att rmse   corr(pre,|err|)  p of corr
         5       0.968      0.887            -0.041     0.3612
        10       0.763      0.888            -0.018     0.6935
        20       0.574      0.865            -0.048     0.2834
        40       0.445      0.878             0.110     0.0137
        80       0.336      0.888             0.014     0.7482

  Going from 5 donors to 80, the pre-period fit improves 2.9x - the picture in the
  paper gets strictly more convincing - while the error on the actual estimate goes
  0.887 -> 0.888 (WORSE).  Within a fixed pool the correlation
  between how good the fit looks and how wrong the answer is stays near zero.

==============================================================================
 4. NEGATIVE: THE CONVEX HULL IS A HARD WALL, AND THE LEVER THAT MOVES IT IS NOT THE OBVIOUS ONE
==============================================================================
The weights are non-negative and sum to one, so the synthetic unit is a convex
combination and cannot leave the donor range: never above the highest donor, never below the
lowest, in any period.  That is a structural guarantee, checked exactly below.  It means a
treated unit sitting above its whole pool by a constant h simply cannot be matched.

The obvious guess is that the estimator then returns tau + h.  It does not.  The optimiser
buys back part of h by tilting the weights toward the highest-level donors and paying for it
in factor fit - so the bias is a FRACTION of h, and the fraction is not a constant.

    shift h   mean att     bias   bias/h   hull excess   pre-RMSPE   in band
        0.0      5.047    0.047    0.000         0.401       0.857     1.000
        1.0      5.154    0.154    0.154         0.711       0.979     1.000
        2.0      5.287    0.287    0.144         1.128       1.289     1.000
        5.0      6.175    1.175    0.235         2.975       2.886     1.000
       10.0      9.574    4.574    0.457         7.242       6.793     1.000
       20.0     19.190   14.190    0.709        17.066      16.213     1.000

  The 'in band' column is 1.000 everywhere: the guarantee holds exactly, in every one
  of 2400 fits.  'hull excess' is the part of the treated path lying outside the
  donor range - computable from the data alone, with no counterfactual in it - and it is
  an honest lower bound on the damage rather than the damage itself.

  Now the two levers, separately, at h = 5.0 (bias to remove: the h=5 row above):

                    held fixed     grown      from        to   bias from   bias to
                30 pre-periods  n_donors         5        80       1.950     0.717
                     20 donors     t_pre        15       240       1.837     0.235

  Widening the pool 16x removes 63% of the bias; lengthening the history 16x removes 87%.
  The donor column is the part worth noticing.  In section 9 - the same estimator, the same
  panel, but a NOISE problem rather than a coverage problem - growing the pool 16x moved the
  error by 3% and was indistinguishable from doing nothing.  Here it removes 63% of a real
  bias.  Donors are not a sample-size lever and they are not useless; they buy hull coverage
  and nothing else.  Which of the two problems you have is not readable off the pre-period
  plot everybody publishes - but it IS readable off the hull excess above, which nobody
  publishes.

==============================================================================
 5. THE RECOMMENDED TEST STATISTIC EARNS ITS KEEP (the one place in this build it does)
==============================================================================
Abadie ranks units by the RATIO of post-period to pre-period fit error, not by the raw
gap.  The reason is section 4: a unit the donors cannot fit has a large gap in every period,
before and after, and a raw-gap ranking reads that as an effect.  Dividing by the pre-period
error cancels it.  Below, the null is true - there is no effect at all - and the treated unit
is simply un-fittable (h = 5).

      h  true effect      stat   p<=0.10   p<=0.20   median p
    0.0          0.0       gap     0.000     0.016      0.714
    0.0          0.0     ratio     0.094     0.188      0.476
    5.0          0.0       gap     0.424     0.564      0.143
    5.0          0.0     ratio     0.036     0.084      0.905
    0.0          5.0       gap     0.970     0.998      0.048
    0.0          5.0     ratio     0.998     0.998      0.048
    5.0          5.0       gap     0.892     0.934      0.048
    5.0          5.0     ratio     0.482     0.630      0.143

  Three things in that table, in order of how much they matter.

  1. On a TRUE NULL with an un-fittable treated unit the raw-gap statistic fires
     0.424 of the time at a nominal 0.10 - it is reading section 4's
     level shift as an effect - while the ratio fires 0.036.  This is
     the one guard in this build that does the job it is sold for.

  2. It is paid for.  With a real effect on an un-fittable unit the ratio finds it
     0.482 of the time against the raw gap's 0.892 -
     it has thrown away the same signal it correctly refused in row 2, because from inside
     the statistic those two situations are identical.

  3. The raw gap is not merely mis-sized, it is inert on a clean null: 0.000
     at a nominal 0.10, median p 0.714.  The placebo units are not
     exchangeable with the treated one - a donor is an extreme point of its own pool and is
     fitted badly by the units left over, while the treated unit is interior and fits well - so
     the reference distribution is drawn from harder problems than the one being tested.
     Dividing by each unit's own pre-period error is what puts them back on the same scale
     (0.094 at 0.10, median 0.476).  The ratio's
     real job is normalisation, not robustness.

  None of this repairs the ESTIMATE.  Section 4's bias is still in the number; the test has
  only stopped calling it an effect.

==============================================================================
 6. NEGATIVE: DROPPING THE SIMPLEX CONSTRAINT BUYS A BETTER FIT AND A WORSE ANSWER
==============================================================================
The constraint w >= 0, sum w = 1 is the whole method.  Regression on the donors with no
constraint is strictly better at the thing the pre-period plot measures, and that is exactly
why it is worse: it extrapolates.

    pre-periods    solver   pre-RMSPE   att rmse  weight sum  neg wts
             12   simplex       0.646      0.830       1.000      0.0
             12       ols       0.000      1.511       0.944      8.4
             30   simplex       0.843      0.593       1.000      0.0
             30       ols       0.585      1.059       0.945      8.7
             60   simplex       0.927      0.479       1.000      0.0
             60       ols       0.836      0.547       0.937      7.5

   12 pre-periods: OLS fits EXACTLY (interpolates) and estimates  1.82x worse
   30 pre-periods: OLS fits          1.44x tighter and estimates  1.79x worse
   60 pre-periods: OLS fits          1.11x tighter and estimates  1.14x worse

  With fewer pre-periods than donors the unconstrained fit passes through every
  pre-period point exactly - a pre-period RMSPE of 0.000, the most convincing
  version of the picture in section 3 - and is the worst estimator in this build.
  The gap closes as history lengthens, which is the honest version of the claim: the
  constraint is a small-sample device, and it is also a device against being fooled
  by your own diagnostic.

==============================================================================
 7. NEGATIVE: A DONOR THAT ALSO GOT TREATED ATTENUATES BY EXACTLY ITS OWN WEIGHT
==============================================================================
The donor pool is assumed untouched by the intervention.  When a neighbouring market
picks up some share gamma of the effect and carries weight w in the fit, that share is
subtracted from the estimate: the synthetic unit rises with the real one.  The prediction
is tau * (1 - sum_j w_j gamma_j), using the weights the optimiser actually chose - which
are not known in advance, so this is not a correction anyone can apply up front.

This is Day 168's finding at a different level: there the contamination was between arms of
one experiment, here it is between the treated unit and its own control group.

    gamma  donors hit   mean att  measured ratio   predicted      gap
      0.0           1      5.006          1.0012      1.0000   0.0012
      0.5           1      4.291          0.8583      0.8571   0.0012
      1.0           1      3.577          0.7153      0.7141   0.0012
      1.0           3      1.767          0.3534      0.3522   0.0012
      0.5           5      2.858          0.5716      0.5704   0.0012

  The gap column is 0.0012 in the uncontaminated row and stays there: it never moves by more
  than 0.00000 across contaminations that destroy two thirds of the effect.  That constant is
  the estimator's own clean bias, not an error in the closed form - the attenuation is fully
  explained by the weights the optimiser happened to choose.
  Nothing in the output flags this.  The pre-period fit is untouched - the contamination is
  post-period by construction - so the credential in section 3 is not merely uninformative
  here, it is perfect while the answer is wrong.

==============================================================================
 8. ANTICIPATION IS THE ONE FAILURE THE PRE-PERIOD PLOT CAN SEE - WHEN IT IS LOUD
==============================================================================
If the market reacted before the official date - an announcement, a leak, firms moving
early - then the last few pre-periods already contain part of the effect, and the optimiser
fits the donors to a partly-treated series.  The received advice is to check the pre-period
fit and to back the date off if it looks wrong.  Below, the screen is given the best possible
form - reject when pre-RMSPE exceeds the 90th percentile of its own clean distribution, a
threshold no real analyst has - and its POWER is measured against the bias it must catch.

  Clean pre-RMSPE 90th percentile (the screen's threshold): 1.0190

    periods   share   mean att     bias  % of effect  screen power
          0    1.00      4.985   -0.015         -0.3         0.100
          2    1.00      4.154   -0.846        -16.9         0.830
          4    1.00      3.734   -1.266        -25.3         0.894
          6    1.00      3.392   -1.608        -32.2         0.930
         10    1.00      2.854   -2.146        -42.9         0.930
          4    0.50      4.333   -0.667        -13.3         0.342
          4    0.25      4.652   -0.348         -7.0         0.150
         10    0.25      4.413   -0.587        -11.7         0.172

  This is the one failure in the build the standard screen actually catches, and the reason
  is structural: anticipation is the only contamination that lands in the pre-period, which
  is the only place the diagnostic can see.  Two periods of it costs -16.9% of the
  effect and gets flagged 0.830 of the time.

  It is caught because it is LOUD, not because the screen is good.  At a quarter of the
  effect leaking early, 4 periods costs -7.0% and is flagged 0.150 -
  barely above the screen's own 0.10 false-alarm rate - and 10 periods costs
  -11.7% at 0.172.  Same shape as Day 167's pre-trends test and Day 168's
  dose-response guard: the diagnostic's power arrives after the damage, it just arrives
  sooner here than it did there.

  Note the sign.  Every bias in this build so far ran positive; this one runs NEGATIVE -
  fitting donors to an already-treated pre-period drags the counterfactual up, and the
  estimate down.  A researcher who suspects anticipation and cannot rule it out does not
  know which direction they are wrong in without knowing which mechanism they have.

==============================================================================
 9. WHICH LEVER: ONE MORE PRE-PERIOD IS WORTH MORE THAN ONE MORE DONOR, UP TO A POINT
==============================================================================
Both are things a researcher can go and get: dig up more history, or widen the pool of
comparison markets.  They are not interchangeable, and the exchange rate is measurable.

  RMSE of the estimate (true effect 5.0):

  pre \ donors           5        10        20        40        80
  8                  0.916     0.984     0.899     0.972     0.947
  15                 0.749     0.766     0.762     0.747     0.786
  30                 0.659     0.656     0.574     0.578     0.558
  60                 0.520     0.508     0.464     0.472     0.448
  120                0.482     0.427     0.389     0.390     0.387

  Reading the corners: 8 pre-periods and 80 donors gives 0.947; 120 pre-periods and 5
  donors gives 0.482.  Along the top row, growing the pool 16x moves the error
  0.916 -> 0.947 (-3%); down the left column, growing the history 15x moves it
  0.916 -> 0.482 (+47%).  The pool has a second job though -
  section 2's p-value floor is 1/(J+1), and no amount of history buys a single rank of it.
